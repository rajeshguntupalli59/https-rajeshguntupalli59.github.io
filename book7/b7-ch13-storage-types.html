Chapter 13: gp2 vs gp3 vs io1 vs io2: Choosing the Right Storage Type

For most AWS RDS workloads running on gp2 today, a single storage type migration to gp3 delivers an immediate 20–23% cost reduction with zero downtime and no application changes. On a 2TB RDS volume, that is roughly $460 per year saved on storage alone — before accounting for the IOPS and throughput improvements that often eliminate the need for provisioned IOPS entirely. This chapter walks through the mechanics of each storage type, the diagnostic queries that reveal whether your current storage is over- or under-provisioned, the math behind migration decisions, and the automation that makes these changes repeatable across dozens of RDS instances without manual intervention.

---

Understanding the Four Storage Types: Pricing and Architecture

AWS RDS offers four EBS-backed storage types. Each is optimized for a different cost-performance trade-off, and the pricing differences between them are large enough to matter at scale.

gp2 (General Purpose SSD, Generation 2)

gp2 is the original general-purpose option. Pricing sits at approximately $0.115 per GB-month in us-east-1. The key architectural constraint is that IOPS are tied directly to volume size: you receive 3 IOPS per GB, with a baseline of 100 IOPS and a hard ceiling of 16,000 IOPS. A 500GB gp2 volume delivers 1,500 IOPS. To reach 16,000 IOPS on gp2, you must provision at least 5,334GB of storage — even if your database is only 400GB in size. Teams frequently over-provision gp2 storage purely for IOPS, paying for unused space.

gp2 also uses a burst credit system for volumes under 1TB. Volumes smaller than 333GB can burst to 3,000 IOPS when credits are available, but sustained workloads exhaust those credits and drop back to baseline. This creates invisible performance cliffs that only appear under load.

gp3 (General Purpose SSD, Generation 3)

gp3 decouples IOPS from storage size. Base price is $0.08 per GB-month — a 30% reduction versus gp2. Every gp3 volume includes 3,000 IOPS and 125 MB/s throughput at no additional charge. IOPS can be independently scaled up to 16,000 for $0.005 per provisioned IOPS-month above the 3,000 baseline, and throughput up to 1,000 MB/s for $0.04 per MB/s-month above 125 MB/s.

For a 1TB volume:

  • → gp2: 1,024GB × $0.115 = **$117.76/month**, delivers 3,072 IOPS
  • → gp3: 1,024GB × $0.08 = **$81.92/month**, delivers 3,000 IOPS (essentially the same), saves **$35.84/month** or **$430/year**

    • For a 2TB volume with no extra IOPS needed:

  • → gp2: 2,048GB × $0.115 = **$235.52/month**
  • → gp3: 2,048GB × $0.08 = **$163.84/month**, saves **$71.68/month** or **$860/year**

    • io1 (Provisioned IOPS SSD, Generation 1)

    io1 is designed for I/O-intensive workloads requiring sustained, consistent IOPS. Storage costs $0.125 per GB-month, and IOPS are independently provisioned at $0.065 per IOPS-month. The IOPS-to-GB ratio is capped at 50:1. A 500GB io1 volume can provision a maximum of 25,000 IOPS. Maximum IOPS is 64,000 on Nitro-based instances.

    The cost model makes io1 expensive at high IOPS. A volume provisioned at 10,000 IOPS on 200GB of storage costs:

  • → Storage: 200 × $0.125 = $25.00
  • → IOPS: 10,000 × $0.065 = $650.00
  • → **Total: $675.00/month**

    • io2 (Provisioned IOPS SSD, Generation 2)

    io2 replaces io1 for most use cases. It offers higher durability (99.999% versus 99.8% for io1), a 500:1 IOPS-to-GB ratio, and tiered pricing that makes high IOPS cheaper than io1 at volume. As of 2024 pricing in us-east-1:

  • → Storage: **$0.125 per GB-month** (same as io1)
  • → IOPS up to 32,000: **$0.065 per IOPS-month**
  • → IOPS from 32,001–64,000: **$0.046 per IOPS-month**
  • → IOPS above 64,000: **$0.032 per IOPS-month**

    • io2 Block Express extends the ceiling to 256,000 IOPS and 4,000 MB/s throughput, available on r6i, m6i, and similar Nitro instances. For any workload currently on io1, migrating to io2 at the same IOPS level costs the same or less and delivers meaningfully higher durability. There is no reason to choose io1 for new deployments.

    Diagnosing Your Current Storage: Are You Over-Provisioned?

    Before making any changes, collect four weeks of actual storage metrics. The goal is to determine three things: actual IOPS consumed, actual throughput consumed, and the percentage of time the storage is at or near its provisioned limit.

    Querying CloudWatch for RDS Storage Metrics

    # Pull 30-day average and peak IOPS for an RDS instance
# Replace DB_INSTANCE_IDENTIFIER with your actual instance ID

DB_ID="prod-postgres-01"
START_TIME=$(date -u -d '30 days ago' +%Y-%m-%dT%H:%M:%SZ)
END_TIME=$(date -u +%Y-%m-%dT%H:%M:%SZ)

echo "=== Read IOPS (Average and Maximum) ==="
aws cloudwatch get-metric-statistics \
  --namespace AWS/RDS \
  --metric-name ReadIOPS \
  --dimensions Name=DBInstanceIdentifier,Value=$DB_ID \
  --start-time $START_TIME \
  --end-time $END_TIME \
  --period 3600 \
  --statistics Average Maximum \
  --output table

echo "=== Write IOPS (Average and Maximum) ==="
aws cloudwatch get-metric-statistics \
  --namespace AWS/RDS \
  --metric-name WriteIOPS \
  --dimensions Name=DBInstanceIdentifier,Value=$DB_ID \
  --start-time $START_TIME \
  --end-time $END_TIME \
  --period 3600 \
  --statistics Average Maximum \
  --output table

echo "=== Read Throughput MB/s ==="
aws cloudwatch get-metric-statistics \
  --namespace AWS/RDS \
  --metric-name ReadThroughput \
  --dimensions Name=DBInstanceIdentifier,Value=$DB_ID \
  --start-time $START_TIME \
  --end-time $END_TIME \
  --period 3600 \
  --statistics Average Maximum \
  --output table

echo "=== Storage Queue Depth (key saturation signal) ==="
aws cloudwatch get-metric-statistics \
  --namespace AWS/RDS \
  --metric-name DiskQueueDepth \
  --dimensions Name=DBInstanceIdentifier,Value=$DB_ID \
  --start-time $START_TIME \
  --end-time $END_TIME \
  --period 3600 \
  --statistics Average Maximum \
  --output table

# Also pull BurstBalance for gp2 volumes (0% = burst exhausted)
echo "=== Burst Balance (gp2 only — 0% means sustained IOPS pressure) ==="
aws cloudwatch get-metric-statistics \
  --namespace AWS/RDS \
  --metric-name BurstBalance \
  --dimensions Name=DBInstanceIdentifier,Value=$DB_ID \
  --start-time $START_TIME \
  --end-time $END_TIME \
  --period 3600 \
  --statistics Average Minimum \
  --output table

    Interpreting the Results

    Three patterns emerge from this data:

    1. Peak IOPS stays below 2,500 on a gp2 volume of any size — this is a candidate for gp3 with no additional IOPS provisioning. The included 3,000 IOPS on gp3 covers the workload entirely.

    2. BurstBalance frequently drops below 20% — the instance is hitting the burst ceiling. If the volume is under 1TB, either the workload genuinely needs more IOPS (evaluate io2), or the volume should be sized to the performance tier it actually needs on gp3 without the credit system.

    3. DiskQueueDepth regularly exceeds 1.0 — storage is a bottleneck. This is not a candidate for cost reduction yet; it needs either more IOPS or a storage type upgrade evaluated for performance, not cost.

    Querying PostgreSQL for I/O Patterns at the Table Level

    -- Identify which tables and indexes drive the most I/O
-- Run on PostgreSQL RDS / Aurora PostgreSQL
-- Requires pg_stat_user_tables to have accumulated data (reset on restart)

SELECT
    schemaname,
    relname AS table_name,
    heap_blks_read,
    heap_blks_hit,
    ROUND(
        100.0 * heap_blks_hit / NULLIF(heap_blks_hit + heap_blks_read, 0),
        2
    ) AS cache_hit_ratio_pct,
    idx_blks_read,
    idx_blks_hit,
    ROUND(
        100.0 * idx_blks_hit / NULLIF(idx_blks_hit + idx_blks_read, 0),
        2
    ) AS index_cache_hit_pct,
    -- Estimated physical I/O operations (blocks not served from shared_buffers)
    (heap_blks_read + idx_blks_read) AS total_physical_reads
FROM
    pg_stat_user_tables
WHERE
    (heap_blks_read + idx_blks_read) > 1000
ORDER BY
    total_physical_reads DESC
LIMIT 25;

-- Companion query: find the most I/O-intensive queries
-- Requires pg_stat_statements extension (enabled by default on RDS)
SELECT
    LEFT(query, 100) AS query_preview,
    calls,
    total_exec_time::BIGINT AS total_ms,
    ROUND((total_exec_time / calls)::NUMERIC, 2) AS avg_ms,
    shared_blks_read,
    shared_blks_hit,
    ROUND(
        100.0 * shared_blks_hit / NULLIF(shared_blks_hit + shared_blks_read, 0),
        2
    ) AS hit_ratio_pct,
    -- Blocks read from disk (not cache) — direct storage pressure
    shared_blks_read AS disk_blocks_read,
    local_blks_read + temp_blks_read AS temp_disk_reads
FROM
    pg_stat_statements
WHERE
    shared_blks_read > 10000
ORDER BY
    shared_blks_read DESC
LIMIT 20;

    A cache hit ratio consistently above 99% across most tables signals that your workload is largely memory-bound — physical IOPS are a secondary concern, and gp3's baseline allocation is almost certainly sufficient.

    Running the Migration Cost Math

    The decision framework for storage type selection follows a simple flowprint based on actual IOPS requirements:

    Peak IOPS needed?
├── < 3,000:         gp3 (no extra IOPS, lowest cost)
├── 3,000 – 16,000:  gp3 with additional provisioned IOPS
├── 16,000 – 64,000: io2
└── > 64,000:        io2 Block Express (requires Nitro instance class)

    The crossover point where io2 becomes cheaper than gp3 with additional IOPS sits around 16,000 IOPS for volumes under 320GB. For larger volumes, the per-GB price difference adds up in gp3's favor until IOPS demands are extreme.
