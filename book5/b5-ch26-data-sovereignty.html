Chapter 26: Data Sovereignty and Residency Compliance

Data sovereignty — the principle that data is subject to the laws and governance structures of the nation where it physically resides — has transformed from a legal abstraction into an operational constraint that every cloud DBA must architect around. GDPR in the European Union, the Personal Data Protection Bill in India, LGPD in Brazil, PIPL in China, and sector-specific mandates like HIPAA and FedRAMP in the United States all impose requirements that translate directly into database placement decisions, replication topology restrictions, encryption key custody, and audit logging configurations. If you've spent your career managing on-premises databases, you controlled data residency by controlling which rack the server sat in. In the cloud, that control is exercised through region selection, availability zone pinning, replication constraints, and — critically — understanding what the managed service does automatically on your behalf that you may not have realized. This chapter maps the compliance landscape to the actual configuration knobs available in AWS RDS, Azure SQL, and Google Cloud SQL, and shows you how to enforce residency guarantees that will survive a regulator's audit.

---

26.1 Understanding the Regulatory Landscape for Cloud DBAs

Before configuring anything, you need to understand what regulators actually care about, because the mandates are more specific — and more operationally demanding — than most DBAs expect until they're sitting across from an auditor.

What regulators want to know:

  • → Where does the data physically reside at rest?
  • → Where does the data travel during replication, backup, and recovery operations?
  • → Who holds the encryption keys, and where are those keys stored?
  • → Who can access the data — including cloud vendor employees?
  • → Can you prove all of the above with durable audit logs?

    • GDPR Article 44 prohibits transfers of personal data to third countries unless specific adequacy decisions or contractual protections are in place. This applies to your database backups. AWS automated backups for RDS are stored in S3 within the same region by default, but cross-region automated backup copies, Read Replicas in other regions, and cross-region snapshot copies are all "data transfers" under a strict GDPR reading. The fact that the transfer is automated and invisible doesn't make it compliant by default — it makes it a trap.

    The sovereignty matrix for cloud DBAs:

    | Requirement | On-Prem Equivalent | Cloud Mechanism |

    |---|---|---|

    | Data at rest in country X | Server physically in country X | Region selection + no cross-region replication |

    | Encryption key custody | HSM in your data center | Customer-managed keys (CMK) with regional KMS |

    | Access logging | Audit plugin, pgaudit | CloudTrail, Azure Audit Logs, Cloud Audit Logs |

    | Vendor employee access restriction | Physical security, OS ACLs | Nitro enclaves, Confidential Computing, access controls |

    | Backup residency | Tape library location | Backup region configuration, cross-region copy policies |

    The managed service assumption problem:

    When you self-managed PostgreSQL, you decided explicitly where every file went. RDS, Azure SQL, and Cloud SQL all make decisions on your behalf — automated backups, transaction log backups, minor version patching, and parameter group replication all involve data movement and access that happens without a specific operator action. Your job as a cloud DBA is to audit every one of those automatic behaviors against your compliance requirements before you're in production.

    A common shock: AWS RDS Multi-AZ with synchronous replication stays within a single region, but the two AZs may be in different cities. For most regulations, this is fine — they're within the same legal jurisdiction. But some financial regulators in specific countries have interpreted "data residency" down to the metropolitan area. Know your specific regulatory language before assuming Multi-AZ is compliant.

    26.2 AWS RDS and Aurora: Region, Replication, and Key Custody

    AWS gives you fine-grained control over data residency, but you must exercise that control explicitly. The defaults are permissive, not restrictive.

    Region selection and subnet architecture:

    Every RDS or Aurora instance is deployed into a VPC subnet, which lives in a specific Availability Zone within a specific region. The region is the top-level residency boundary. For EU data subject to GDPR, eu-west-1 (Ireland), eu-central-1 (Frankfurt), eu-south-1 (Milan), and eu-north-1 (Stockholm) are all within the EU, but they are different legal jurisdictions for some regulatory interpretations. German financial regulators and French data authorities have historically preferred data to remain within their national borders — not just the EU broadly.

    # Create an RDS PostgreSQL instance explicitly pinned to eu-central-1 (Frankfurt)
# and document the region choice in tags for audit purposes

aws rds create-db-instance \
  --db-instance-identifier prod-gdpr-postgres \
  --db-instance-class db.r6g.2xlarge \
  --engine postgres \
  --engine-version 15.4 \
  --master-username dbadmin \
  --master-user-password "$(aws secretsmanager get-secret-value \
    --secret-id prod/postgres/masterpassword \
    --region eu-central-1 \
    --query SecretString \
    --output text)" \
  --db-subnet-group-name prod-eu-central-private-subnet-group \
  --vpc-security-group-ids sg-0a1b2c3d4e5f67890 \
  --storage-encrypted \
  --kms-key-id arn:aws:kms:eu-central-1:123456789012:key/mrk-abc123 \
  --backup-retention-period 7 \
  --preferred-backup-window "02:00-03:00" \
  --no-publicly-accessible \
  --region eu-central-1 \
  --tags \
    Key=DataResidency,Value=EU-Germany \
    Key=ComplianceFramework,Value=GDPR \
    Key=DataClassification,Value=PersonalData \
    Key=RegionLocked,Value=true

    Customer-managed keys and key residency:

    This is where most DBAs underestimate the complexity. AWS KMS regional keys are region-specific — a key created in eu-central-1 cannot be used to encrypt data in us-east-1. This is good for residency, because it forces you to use a region-local key for your data. But AWS KMS Multi-Region Keys (MRKs) can replicate key material across regions, which is a compliance problem if your regulation requires key material to remain in-country.

    For the strictest sovereignty requirements, use AWS CloudHSM deployed in your target region, then configure KMS to use CloudHSM as its custom key store. Key material never leaves the HSM, and the HSM is physically in a specific AWS data center within your region.

    # Verify that an existing RDS instance is using a CMK (not AWS-managed key)
# and confirm the key is regional, not a multi-region key

aws rds describe-db-instances \
  --db-instance-identifier prod-gdpr-postgres \
  --region eu-central-1 \
  --query 'DBInstances[0].{
    KmsKeyId: KmsKeyId,
    StorageEncrypted: StorageEncrypted,
    DBInstanceArn: DBInstanceArn
  }'

# Check if the KMS key is a multi-region key (MultiRegion: true would be a compliance flag)
aws kms describe-key \
  --key-id arn:aws:kms:eu-central-1:123456789012:key/mrk-abc123 \
  --region eu-central-1 \
  --query 'KeyMetadata.{
    KeyId: KeyId,
    MultiRegion: MultiRegion,
    KeyState: KeyState,
    KeyManager: KeyManager,
    Origin: Origin
  }'
# Origin: AWS_KMS = key material in KMS
# Origin: EXTERNAL = you imported key material (highest control)
# Origin: AWS_CLOUDHSM = key material in CloudHSM (strongest for compliance)

    Preventing cross-region replication and backup copies:

    The most dangerous residency violation is an automated process that no one remembers setting up. Use AWS Service Control Policies (SCPs) at the AWS Organizations level to deny cross-region RDS actions for accounts handling sovereign data.

    # SCP to prevent cross-region RDS snapshot copies OUT of eu-central-1
# Apply this to the OU containing GDPR-scoped accounts
# Save as gdpr-rds-residency-scp.json and attach via Organizations

cat > gdpr-rds-residency-scp.json << 'EOF'
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyRDSCrossRegionSnapshotCopy",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": [
        "rds:CopyDBSnapshot",
        "rds:CopyDBClusterSnapshot"
      ],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:RequestedRegion": "eu-central-1"
        }
      }
    },
    {
      "Sid": "DenyRDSCrossRegionReadReplica",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": [
        "rds:CreateDBInstanceReadReplica"
      ],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:RequestedRegion": "eu-central-1"
        }
      }
    },
    {
      "Sid": "DenyRDSGlobalCluster",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": [
        "rds:CreateGlobalCluster",
        "rds:AddMemberToGlobalCluster"
      ],
      "Resource": "*"
    }
  ]
}
EOF

# Attach to the target organizational unit
aws organizations create-policy \
  --name "GDPR-RDS-Residency-Enforcement" \
  --description "Prevents RDS data from leaving eu-central-1" \
  --content file://gdpr-rds-residency-scp.json \
  --type SERVICE_CONTROL_POLICY

aws organizations attach-policy \
  --policy-id p-examplepolicyid \
  --target-id ou-exampleouid

    Aurora Global Database and sovereignty:

    Aurora Global Database provides sub-second replication across regions for disaster recovery — which is architecturally attractive but potentially legally problematic. If you operate a Global Database with a primary in eu-central-1 and a secondary in us-east-1, you have EU personal data replicating to US servers. This may violate GDPR without appropriate data transfer mechanisms (Standard Contractual Clauses, Binding Corporate Rules) in place. The SCP above prevents this at the organizational level. If you need cross-region DR within compliant boundaries (e.g., eu-central-1 to eu-west-1), that's permissible for most EU regulations — but document it explicitly with your Data Protection Officer.

    26.3 Azure SQL: Geo-Replication, Paired Regions, and Data Boundary

    Microsoft has invested heavily in its Azure Data Boundary commitment — a contractual and technical guarantee that customer data stored and processed in the EU stays within the EU. For cloud DBAs, this is useful, but it doesn't eliminate the need for explicit configuration.

    Azure SQL geo-redundant backup storage:

    When you create an Azure SQL Database, the default backup storage redundancy is Geo — meaning your backups are replicated to the Azure paired region automatically. For West Europe (Netherlands), the paired region is North Europe (Ireland). Both are in the EU, so GDPR compliance is maintained for EU data subjects. But if you're in Brazil South, the paired region is Brazil Southeast. Both are in Brazil, satisfying LGPD. Know your paired regions before accepting the default.

    For regulations that prohibit any cross-location replication (some government cloud mandates), set backup storage redundancy to Zone or Local:

    # Create Azure SQL Database with zone-redundant backup storage
# (no cross-region backup replication)

az sql db create \
  --resource-group rg-prod-eu \
  --server sql-prod-westeurope \
  --name gdpr-production-db \
  --edition GeneralPurpose \
  --compute-model Provisioned \
  --family Gen5 \
  --capacity 8 \
  --zone-redundant true \
  --backup-storage-redundancy Zone \
  --location westeurope

# Verify the backup storage redundancy setting
az sql db show \
  --resource-group rg-prod-eu \
  --server sql-prod-westeurope \
  --name gdpr-production-db \
  --query '{
    name: name,
    location: location,
    backupStorageRedundancy: currentBackupStorageRedundancy,
    zoneRedundant: zoneRedundant
  }'

    Azure SQL Always Encrypted for column-level sovereignty:

    Always Encrypted is the Azure SQL equivalent of a technical guarantee that the cloud provider — including Microsoft employees — cannot read sensitive column values. Encryption and decryption happen exclusively in the client application. The database engine stores and retrieves ciphertext and never sees plaintext values. For regulated industries where vendor access is the residency concern (not geographic placement), this is a powerful tool.

    Always Encrypted uses a two-tier key hierarchy: Column Master Keys (CMK) stored in Azure Key Vault, and Column Encryption Keys (CEK) encrypted with the CMK. Your Key Vault can be configured with HSM-backed keys for the strongest custody guarantee.

    -- Configure Always Encrypted on Azure SQL for a PII table
-- This runs in SSMS or Azure Data Studio with the Always Encrypted wizard
-- or can be scripted via PowerShell/az CLI

-- Step 1: Create the Column Master Key reference in the database
-- (actual key material is in Azure Key Vault, not the database)
CREATE COLUMN MASTER KEY CMK_AzureKeyVault
WITH (
    KEY_STORE_PROVIDER_NAME = 'AZURE_KEY_VAULT',
    KEY_PATH = 'https://prod-keyvault-eu.vault.azure.net/keys/AlwaysEncryptedCMK/abc123def456'
);

-- Step 2: Create the Column Encryption Key, encrypted with the CMK
CREATE COLUMN ENCRYPTION KEY CEK_PersonalData
WITH VALUES (
    COLUMN_MASTER_KEY = CMK_AzureKeyVault,
    ALGORITHM = 'RSA_OAEP',
    ENCRYPTED_VALUE = 0x016E...  -- Generated by client tooling with access to Key Vault
);

-- Step 3: Create the table with encrypted columns
-- Deterministic encryption: allows equality comparisons (for WHERE clauses)
-- Randomized encryption: stronger, but no WHERE/ORDER BY/GROUP BY on the column
CREATE TABLE dbo.CustomerPersonalData (
    CustomerID      INT IDENTITY(1,1) PRIMARY KEY,
    FullName        NVARCHAR(200) COLLATE Latin1_General_BIN2
                    ENCRYPTED WITH (
                        COLUMN_ENCRYPTION_KEY = CEK_PersonalData,
                        ENCRYPTION_TYPE = Deterministic,
                        ALGORITHM = 'AEAD_AES_256_CBC_HMAC_SHA_256'
                    ) NOT NULL,
    NationalID      NVARCHAR(20) COLLATE Latin1_General_BIN2
                    ENCRYPTED WITH (
                        COLUMN_ENCRYPTION_KEY = CEK_PersonalData,
                        ENCRYPTION_TYPE = Randomized,
                        ALGORITHM = 'AEAD_AES_256_CBC_HMAC_SHA_256'
                    ) NULL,
    DateOfBirth     DATE
                    ENCRYPTED WITH (
                        COLUMN_ENCRYPTION_KEY = CEK_PersonalData,
                        ENCRYPTION_TYPE = Randomized,
                        ALGORITHM = 'AEAD_AES_256_CBC_HMAC_SHA_256'
                    ) NULL,
    CreatedAt       DATETIME2 NOT NULL DEFAULT SYSUTCDATETIME()
);

-- The database engine sees only ciphertext in FullName, NationalID, DateOfBirth
-- Azure support engineers with direct server access cannot read these values
-- SELECT * FROM dbo.CustomerPersonalData will return encrypted bytes to non-AE clients

    Azure Policy for residency enforcement:

    Like AWS SCPs, Azure Policy can enforce residency at the management group or subscription level, preventing resources from being created in non-compliant regions.

    # Create and assign an Azure Policy that restricts SQL resources to West Europe only
# Built-in policy: "Allowed locations" - policy definition ID varies by tenant

# First, get the built-in policy definition for "Allowed locations"
POLICY_DEF_ID=$(az policy definition list \
  --query "[?displayName=='Allowed locations'].id" \
  --output tsv)

# Assign the policy to a resource group, restricting to westeurope only
az policy assignment create \
  --name "sql-residency-westeurope" \
  --display-name "SQL Resources Must Stay in West Europe" \
  --scope "/subscriptions/sub-id-here/resourceGroups/rg-prod-eu" \
  --policy "$POLICY_DEF_ID" \
  --params '{
    "listOfAllowedLocations": {
      "value": ["westeurope"]
    }
  }' \
  --enforcement-mode Default

# Verify the assignment
az policy assignment show \
  --name "sql-residency-westeurope" \
  --scope "/subscriptions/sub-id-here/resourceGroups/rg-prod-eu" \
  --query '{
    name: name,
    enforcementMode: enforcementMode,
    parameters: parameters
  }'

    Active Geo-Replication and legal review:

    Azure SQL Active Geo-Replication lets you create up to four readable secondary databases in any region. Unlike AWS Global Clusters, this is configured per-database rather than per-cluster, giving you finer control. But the sovereignty risk is identical: verify that all secondary regions are within legally compliant jurisdictions before enabling it. For financial institutions in the EU operating under DORA (Digital Operational Resilience Act), cross-region replication may be required for resilience — confirm the current DORA text and your regulator's guidance on permitted DR regions before finalizing a topology, as ICT resilience regulation in this area continues to evolve.

    26.4 Google Cloud SQL, AlloyDB, and Spanner: Residency Controls

    Google Cloud's approach to data residency spans three products with significantly different residency capabilities.

    Cloud SQL: regional by design, with explicit controls:

    Cloud SQL instances are regional. Unlike Cloud Spanner, they don't span regions by default. Cross-region read replicas are opt-in, and automated backups stay in the same region as the instance. For most sovereignty requirements, Cloud SQL's defaults are residency-safe, but verify them explicitly.

    # Create a Cloud SQL PostgreSQL instance with explicit region pinning
# and verify residency configuration

gcloud sql instances create prod-gdpr-postgres \
  --database-version=POSTGRES_15 \
  --tier=db-custom-8-32768 \
  --region=europe-west3 \
  --availability-type=REGIONAL \
  --storage-type=SSD \
  --storage-size=500GB \
  --storage-auto-increase \
  --backup-start-time=02:00 \
  --enable-bin-log \
  --retained-backups-count=7 \
  --deletion-protection \
  --no-assign-ip \
  --network=projects/my-project/global/networks/prod-vpc \
  --labels=data-residency=eu-germany,compliance=gdpr,data-classification=personal

# Verify the instance location and backup configuration
gcloud sql instances describe prod-gdpr-postgres \
  --format="yaml(name,region,settings.backupConfiguration,settings.locationPreference)"

# List all replicas for this instance to verify none are cross-region
gcloud sql instances list \
  --filter="masterInstanceName:prod-gdpr-postgres" \
  --format="table(name,region,databaseVersion,state)"

    Assured Workloads for regulatory compliance:

    Google Cloud Assured Workloads is the platform-level mechanism for enforcing data residency and access controls for regulated workloads. It restricts which Google Cloud services and regions can be used within a folder, enforces organization policies, and — for sovereign cloud configurations — restricts Google personnel access to a subset of employees in specific jurisdictions.

    For EU sovereign requirements, the "EU Regions and Support" configuration restricts your folder to EU regions and ensures that Google support staff who can access your data are located in the EU. This addresses the vendor employee access concern that regulators increasingly scrutinize.

    # Enable Assured Workloads folder for EU sovereign configuration
# This requires Organization Admin permissions

gcloud assured workloads create \
  --organization=123456789 \
  --location=europe-west3 \
  --display-name="GDPR-Production-Workload" \
  --compliance-regime=EU_REGIONS_AND_SUPPORT \
  --billing-account=billingAccounts/ABCDEF-123456-GHIJKL \
  --resource-settings='[
    {
      "resourceType": "CONSUMER_PROJECT",
      "displayName": "gdpr-prod-project"
    }
  ]'

# After creation, verify the organization policies applied to the folder
gcloud resource-manager org-policies list \
  --folder=FOLDER_ID_FROM_ABOVE \
  --format="table(constraint,spec.rules[0].enforce)"
# Look for:
# constraints/gcp.resourceLocations - restricts to EU regions
# constraints/iam.allowedPolicyMemberDomains - restricts IAM membership
# constraints/gcp.restrictServiceUsage - restricts to approved services

    Cloud Spanner and the multi-region trap:

    Spanner's multi-region configurations are architecturally elegant for global applications but are a sovereignty minefield. A nam6 (North America multi-region) configuration spans multiple US states. A eur3 configuration spans Netherlands and Belgium — both EU member states, so GDPR-compliant. But global configurations span multiple continents, and asia1 spans Tokyo and Osaka.

    The key residency risk with Spanner is the witness replica. In a multi-region configuration, Spanner places leader replicas in some regions and witness replicas in others as defined by the named configuration. The witness replica location is fixed per configuration and may not be in your desired jurisdiction — consult the current Spanner instance-configs documentation (or gcloud spanner instance-configs describe) for the exact replica placement of any multi-region config you're evaluating, including eur3. Before choosing any multi-region Spanner configuration, verify every replica placement against your regulatory requirements, not just the primary region.

    If you need single-region Spanner for strict sovereignty, use a regional instance — you sacrifice global reads and multi-region writes but gain unambiguous data residency.

    # Create a single-region Spanner instance for strict residency
# (regional, not multi-region - no data leaves europe-west3)

gcloud spanner instances create prod-sovereign-spanner \
  --config=regional-europe-west3 \
  --description="GDPR-compliant single-region Spanner instance" \
  --nodes=3 \
  --project=gdpr-production-project

# Verify the instance configuration - inspect which regions are involved
gcloud spanner instance-configs describe regional-europe-west3 \
  --format="yaml(displayName,replicas)"

# List all databases on this instance
gcloud spanner databases list \
  --instance=prod-sovereign-spanner \
  --format="table(name,state,encryptionInfo.encryptionType)"

# Create a database with customer-managed encryption
gcloud spanner databases create customer-data \
  --instance=prod-sovereign-spanner \
  --kms-key=projects/gdpr-production-project/locations/europe-west3/keyRings/spanner-keyring/cryptoKeys/spanner-key

    26.5 Audit Logging for Residency Compliance

    Regulatory frameworks don't just require that you implement residency controls — they require that you prove those controls are in effect and working. Audit logging for sovereignty compliance is different from performance monitoring or application debugging. It must capture specific events: data access, schema changes, privilege escalations, failed access attempts, and — for the highest-sensitivity requirements — every query that touches regulated columns.

    AWS RDS audit logging with CloudWatch Logs:

    RDS PostgreSQL ships logs to CloudWatch Logs within the same region as the instance — ensuring the audit trail itself satisfies residency requirements. For PostgreSQL, pgaudit is the standard audit extension, providing object-level and statement-level logging.

    -- Enable pgaudit on RDS PostgreSQL
-- First, add pgaudit to shared_preload_libraries via parameter group
-- Then configure audit scope per session or globally

-- Verify pgaudit is loaded
SHOW shared_preload_libraries;

-- Set global audit logging (in parameter group: pgaudit.log = 'ddl, write, role')
-- Or set per-session for fine-grained control:
SET pgaudit.log = 'all';
SET pgaudit.log_catalog = on;
SET pgaudit.log_relation = on;
SET pgaudit.log_parameter = on;  -- logs bind parameters — important for PII audits

-- Audit-specific objects only (table-level granularity)
-- Useful when you want to audit only tables containing personal data
CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS pgaudit;

-- Enable object auditing for a specific table
SELECT pgaudit.set_log('SELECT,INSERT,UPDATE,DELETE', 'public', 'CustomerPersonalData');
    -- SQL Server equivalent: enable server audit for Azure SQL and RDS SQL Server
-- Azure SQL uses built-in auditing; RDS SQL Server requires SQL Server Audit

-- Create a server audit (SQL Server on-premises equivalent approach for RDS)
CREATE SERVER AUDIT GDPR_Audit
TO FILE (
    FILEPATH = 'D:\SQLAudit\',
    MAXSIZE = 100 MB,
    MAX_ROLLOVER_FILES = 20,
    RESERVE_DISK_SPACE = OFF
)
WITH (
    QUEUE_DELAY = 1000,
    ON_FAILURE = CONTINUE
);

-- Create database audit specification targeting PII tables
CREATE DATABASE AUDIT SPECIFICATION GDPR_DB_Audit
FOR SERVER AUDIT GDPR_Audit
ADD (SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE
     ON dbo.CustomerPersonalData BY public),
ADD (SCHEMA_OBJECT_ACCESS_GROUP),
ADD (DATABASE_ROLE_MEMBER_CHANGE_GROUP),
ADD (DATABASE_PERMISSION_CHANGE_GROUP)
WITH (STATE = ON);

ALTER SERVER AUDIT GDPR_Audit WITH (STATE = ON);

    For Azure SQL, the built-in auditing feature writes to Azure Blob Storage, Log Analytics, or Event Hub. Configure it to write to a storage account in the same region as the database — do not allow cross-region audit log storage, which would create a secondary residency concern.

    # Enable Azure SQL auditing to a storage account in the same region
az sql db audit-policy update \
  --resource-group rg-prod-eu \
  --server sql-prod-westeurope \
  --name gdpr-production-db \
  --state Enabled \
  --storage-account "prodauditwesteurope" \
  --retention-days 90 \
  --storage-endpoint "https://prodauditwesteurope.blob.core.windows.net" \
  --storage-key "$(az storage account keys list \
    --account-name prodauditwesteurope \
    --resource-group rg-prod-eu \
    --query '[0].value' \
    --output tsv)"

# Verify auditing configuration
az sql db audit-policy show \
  --resource-group rg-prod-eu \
  --server sql-prod-westeurope \
  --name gdpr-production-db \
  --query '{
    state: state,
    storageEndpoint: storageEndpoint,
    retentionDays: retentionDays,
    auditActionsAndGroups: auditActionsAndGroups
  }'

    Google Cloud SQL audit logging with Cloud Audit Logs:

    Cloud SQL integrates with Cloud Audit Logs for data access events. By default, Admin Activity logs are always enabled. Data Access logs (which capture SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE) must be explicitly enabled at the project or organization level.

    # Enable Data Access audit logs for Cloud SQL at the project level
# This captures all DML and DDL operations for compliance evidence

gcloud projects get-iam-policy gdpr-production-project \
  --format=json > current-policy.json

# Add Data Access log configuration to the policy
cat > audit-log-patch.json << 'EOF'
{
  "auditConfigs": [
    {
      "service": "cloudsql.googleapis.com",
      "auditLogConfigs": [
        { "logType": "ADMIN_READ" },
        { "logType": "DATA_READ" },
        { "logType": "DATA_WRITE" }
      ]
    }
  ]
}
EOF

# Apply via org-policy (preferred for organization-wide enforcement)
gcloud organizations add-iam-policy-binding 123456789 \
  --member="allUsers" \
  --role="roles/logging.viewer" \
  --condition=None

# More precisely: set audit log config at org level using gcloud logging
gcloud logging settings update \
  --organization=123456789 \
  --log-filter='resource.type="cloudsql_database"' \
  --storage-location=europe-west3

    Building a cross-platform residency audit dashboard:

    For organizations running regulated workloads across multiple clouds — common for multinationals hedging vendor dependency — you need a unified view of residency compliance. The following shows how to structure a compliance query that runs as a scheduled check, pulling evidence that all databases remain in their declared regions with the expected encryption and replication constraints.

    #!/bin/bash
# residency-compliance-check.sh
# Run this as a scheduled job (daily minimum, hourly for high-assurance environments)
# Outputs a JSON compliance report for ingestion into SIEM or compliance tooling

OUTPUT_FILE="residency-check-$(date +%Y%m%d-%H%M%S).json"
EXPECTED_REGION_AWS="eu-central-1"
EXPECTED_REGION_GCP="europe-west3"

echo "{ \"timestamp\": \"$(date -u +%Y-%m-%dT%H:%M:%SZ)\", \"findings\": [" > "$OUTPUT_FILE"

# ---- AWS RDS Check ----
echo "Checking AWS RDS instances..."

aws rds describe-db-instances \
  --region "$EXPECTED_REGION_AWS" \
  --query 'DBInstances[?Tags[?Key==`ComplianceFramework` && Value==`GDPR`]].{
    id: DBInstanceIdentifier,
    region: AvailabilityZone,
    encrypted: StorageEncrypted,
    kmsKey: KmsKeyId,
    multiAZ: MultiAZ,
    publiclyAccessible: PubliclyAccessible,
    backupRetention: BackupRetentionPeriod,
    readReplicas: ReadReplicaDBInstanceIdentifiers
  }' \
  --output json | python3 -c "
import sys, json
instances = json.load(sys.stdin)
for inst in instances:
    finding = {
        'platform': 'AWS_RDS',
        'resource': inst['id'],
        'checks': {
            'encrypted': inst['encrypted'],
            'not_public': not inst['publiclyAccessible'],
            'backup_retention_ok': inst['backupRetention'] >= 7,
            'no_cross_region_replicas': len(inst.get('readReplicas', [])) == 0
        }
    }
    finding['compliant'] = all(finding['checks'].values())
    print(json.dumps(finding) + ',')
" >> "$OUTPUT_FILE"

    26.6 Data Classification and Residency-Aware Schema Design

    Residency compliance is not binary across your entire database — it applies to specific data elements. A customer's shipping address is personal data subject to GDPR. The timestamp of a page view might not be. Designing schemas with residency awareness means knowing which columns carry sovereignty obligations and ensuring those obligations are enforceable at the database layer, not just in application logic.

    Tagging columns with classification metadata (PostgreSQL):

    PostgreSQL doesn't have a native column classification system, but you can implement one using pg_description (the comment table) and a naming convention enforced by DDL triggers or linting tools in your CI/CD pipeline.

    -- PostgreSQL: document data classification in column comments
-- This creates a queryable audit trail of which columns hold regulated data

CREATE TABLE customers (
    customer_id     BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
    email           TEXT NOT NULL,
    full_name       TEXT,
    date_of_birth   DATE,
    national_id     TEXT,
    country_code    CHAR(2) NOT NULL,
    created_at      TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
    updated_at      TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW()
);

-- Tag columns with classification metadata via COMMENT
COMMENT ON COLUMN customers.email IS
  '{"classification":"PERSONAL_DATA","gdpr_category":"contact","lawful_basis":"contract","retention_days":2555}';

COMMENT ON COLUMN customers.full_name IS
  '{"classification":"PERSONAL_DATA","gdpr_category":"identity","lawful_basis":"contract","retention_days":2555}';

COMMENT ON COLUMN customers.date_of_birth IS
  '{"classification":"SENSITIVE_PERSONAL_DATA","gdpr_category":"identity","lawful_basis":"legal_obligation","retention_days":3650}';

COMMENT ON COLUMN customers.national_id IS
  '{"classification":"SENSITIVE_PERSONAL_DATA","gdpr_category":"government_id","lawful_basis":"legal_obligation","retention_days":3650}';

COMMENT ON COLUMN customers.country_code IS
  '{"classification":"OPERATIONAL","gdpr_category":"none","lawful_basis":"none","retention_days":-1}';

-- Query to get all regulated columns across all tables
-- Use this to drive access control reviews and encryption audits
SELECT
    n.nspname                               AS schema_name,
    c.relname                               AS table_name,
    a.attname                               AS column_name,
    pg_catalog.col_description(c.oid, a.attnum)::jsonb AS classification
FROM
    pg_catalog.pg_attribute a
    JOIN pg_catalog.pg_class c ON c.oid = a.attrelid
    JOIN pg_catalog.pg_namespace n ON n.oid = c.relnamespace
WHERE
    a.attnum > 0
    AND NOT a.attisdropped
    AND pg_catalog.col_description(c.oid, a.attnum) IS NOT NULL
    AND pg_catalog.col_description(c.oid, a.attnum) LIKE '%"classification"%'
    AND (pg_catalog.col_description(c.oid, a.attnum)::jsonb->>'classification')
        IN ('PERSONAL_DATA', 'SENSITIVE_PERSONAL_DATA')
ORDER BY
    schema_name, table_name, column_name;
    -- SQL Server equivalent: use extended properties for column classification
-- This integrates with SQL Server's built-in classification infrastructure

-- Add data classification extended properties
EXEC sys.sp_addextendedproperty
    @name = N'sys_information_type_name',
    @value = N'National Identification Number',
    @level0type = N'SCHEMA', @level0name = N'dbo',
    @level1type = N'TABLE',  @level1name = N'Customers',
    @level2type = N'COLUMN', @level2name = N'NationalID';

EXEC sys.sp_addextendedproperty
    @name = N'sys_sensitivity_label_name',
    @value = N'Highly Confidential',
    @level0type = N'SCHEMA', @level0name = N'dbo',
    @level1type = N'TABLE',  @level1name = N'Customers',
    @level2type = N'COLUMN', @level2name = N'NationalID';

-- Azure SQL / SQL Server 2019+: use the built-in classification feature
-- This integrates with Microsoft Purview and Azure Defender for SQL
ADD SENSITIVITY CLASSIFICATION TO
    dbo.Customers.NationalID
WITH (
    LABEL = 'Highly Confidential',
    LABEL_ID = 'bf91e08c-f4f0-478a-b016-25164b2a65ff',
    INFORMATION_TYPE = 'National Identification Number',
    INFORMATION_TYPE_ID = '3dc733e8-e8a8-4c7c-b285-c6bdc9acacdf',
    RANK = CRITICAL
);

-- Query all classified columns for compliance reporting
SELECT
    schema_name(t.schema_id)            AS schema_name,
    t.name                              AS table_name,
    c.name                              AS column_name,
    sc.information_type                 AS information_type,
    sc.label                            AS sensitivity_label,
    sc.rank_desc                        AS rank
FROM
    sys.sensitivity_classifications sc
    JOIN sys.columns c ON sc.major_id = c.object_id AND sc.minor_id = c.column_id
    JOIN sys.tables t ON c.object_id = t.object_id
ORDER BY
    sc.rank DESC, schema_name, table_name, column_name;

    Row-level residency for multinational databases:

    Some architectures store data for multiple jurisdictions in a single database cluster — a common pattern for SaaS providers serving customers in both the EU and the US. If you cannot shard by region at the database level, row-level security becomes a compensating control that limits access to data based on its jurisdictional tag.

    -- PostgreSQL: Row-Level Security for jurisdiction-partitioned data
-- Ensures application sessions tagged for one jurisdiction cannot
-- access another jurisdiction's personal data

ALTER TABLE customers ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;

-- Policy: sessions must declare their jurisdiction via a session variable
-- Application sets: SET app.jurisdiction = 'EU' before executing queries

CREATE POLICY jurisdiction_isolation ON customers
    USING (
        country_code = ANY(
            CASE current_setting('app.jurisdiction', TRUE)
                WHEN 'EU'  THEN ARRAY['DE','FR','ES','IT','NL','BE','PL','SE','AT','DK','FI','PT','IE','GR','CZ','RO','HU','SK','BG','HR','LT','LV','EE','SI','CY','LU','MT']
                WHEN 'US'  THEN ARRAY['US']
                WHEN 'BR'  THEN ARRAY['BR']
                ELSE ARRAY[]::CHAR(2)[]
            END
        )
    );

-- Allow superusers to bypass RLS for administrative operations
-- (administrative access should itself be audited and jurisdictionally constrained)
ALTER TABLE customers FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
-- FORCE means even table owner is subject to RLS; remove for DBA bypass if needed

-- Test the policy:
SET app.jurisdiction = 'EU';
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM customers;  -- Returns only EU country_code rows

SET app.jurisdiction = 'US';
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM customers;  -- Returns only US rows

    26.7 Right to Erasure and the Database Engineering Challenge

    GDPR Article 17 and equivalent provisions in LGPD, PIPL, and CCPA establish the right to erasure — the obligation to delete a data subject's personal data upon request within defined timeframes. For a cloud DBA, this is one of the most architecturally demanding compliance requirements because deletion in a relational database is not truly deletion: it persists in transaction logs, MVCC dead tuples, automated backups, read replica lag windows, and any audit logs that recorded the original insert.

    The erasure surface area in managed databases:

    When you execute DELETE FROM customers WHERE customer_id = 12345, the following still contain the data:

    1. PostgreSQL MVCC dead tuples — until VACUUM reclaims them. With autovacuum running, this may be minutes to hours.

    2. WAL / transaction logs — the WAL record of the delete contains the old row values (before-image). WAL segments are retained for replication lag tolerance; WAL archiving for PITR means they may persist for your full backup retention period.

    3. Read replicas — the replica applies the WAL and creates its own dead tuples, which must also be vacuumed.

    4. Automated backups — backups taken before the delete permanently contain the deleted record until the backup expires.

    5. Audit logs — if pgaudit or SQL Server Audit logged the original INSERT with parameter logging enabled, the personal data is in the audit log.

    6. Application caches — outside the database, but part of the erasure obligation.

    graph TD
    D[DELETE executed
    on primary] --> T[MVCC dead tuple
    until VACUUM]
    D --> W[WAL record
    before-image of row]
    W --> R[Read replicas
    apply WAL, create own dead tuples]
    W --> A[PITR archive
    persists for retention window]
    D2[Original INSERT] --> L[Audit log
    if parameter logging was on]
    D2 --> B[Pre-delete backups
    persist until expiry]
    D --> C[Application caches
    outside the database]

    *A single row deletion leaves the data recoverable from at least six other locations — the DELETE statement is the start of the erasure obligation, not the end of it.*

    The pseudonymization strategy:

    For production databases where immediate physical deletion is operationally risky, pseudonymization of personal data upon erasure request is an accepted GDPR technique — replacing identifying values with irreversible tokens while retaining the record structure for referential integrity and analytics.

    -- PostgreSQL: Pseudonymization procedure for right-to-erasure requests
-- Replaces personal data with deterministic tokens that cannot be reversed
-- Maintains referential integrity and audit trail of the erasure event

CREATE TABLE erasure_requests (
    request_id      BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
    customer_id     BIGINT NOT NULL,
    requested_at    TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
    completed_at    TIMESTAMPTZ,
    requested_by    TEXT NOT NULL,   -- Legal team identifier, not personal data
    status          TEXT NOT NULL DEFAULT 'PENDING'
        CHECK (status IN ('PENDING','PROCESSING','COMPLETED','FAILED'))
);

CREATE OR REPLACE PROCEDURE process_erasure_request(
    p_customer_id   BIGINT,
    p_request_id    BIGINT
)
LANGUAGE plpgsql
AS $$
DECLARE
    v_erasure_token TEXT;
BEGIN
    -- Generate a non-reversible erasure token for audit evidence
    v_erasure_token := 'ERASED-' || encode(
        digest(p_customer_id::TEXT || NOW()::TEXT, 'sha256'),
        'hex'
    );

    -- Pseudonymize all personal data columns
    -- Replace with the erasure token — clearly marked as erased, not null
    UPDATE customers
    SET
        email         = v_erasure_token || '@erased.invalid',
        full_name     = '[ERASED]',
        date_of_birth = NULL,
        national_id   = NULL,
        updated_at    = NOW()
    WHERE customer_id = p_customer_id;

    -- Pseudonymize related tables containing personal data
    UPDATE customer_addresses
    SET
        street_line_1 = '[ERASED]',
        street_line_2 = NULL,
        city          = '[ERASED]',
        postal_code   = '[ERASED]'
    WHERE customer_id = p_customer_id;

    UPDATE customer_contact_preferences
    SET
        phone_number  = NULL,
        mobile_number = NULL
    WHERE customer_id = p_customer_id;

    -- Record completion in the erasure audit table
    UPDATE erasure_requests
    SET
        completed_at = NOW(),
        status       = 'COMPLETED'
    WHERE request_id = p_request_id;

    -- Log the erasure event (the token, not the original data)
    INSERT INTO erasure_audit_log (
        customer_id, erasure_token, erased_at, request_id
    ) VALUES (
        p_customer_id, v_erasure_token, NOW(), p_request_id
    );

    COMMIT;

EXCEPTION WHEN OTHERS THEN
    UPDATE erasure_requests
    SET status = 'FAILED'
    WHERE request_id = p_request_id;
    RAISE;
END;
$$;
    -- SQL Server equivalent: Erasure procedure using UPDATE with masked values
-- Compatible with Azure SQL and RDS SQL Server

CREATE PROCEDURE dbo.ProcessErasureRequest
    @CustomerID     INT,
    @RequestID      BIGINT,
    @RequestedBy    NVARCHAR(200)
AS
BEGIN
    SET NOCOUNT ON;
    BEGIN TRY
        BEGIN TRANSACTION;

        DECLARE @ErasureToken NVARCHAR(100) =
            'ERASED-' + CONVERT(NVARCHAR(64),
                HASHBYTES('SHA2_256',
                    CAST(@CustomerID AS NVARCHAR(20)) +
                    CONVERT(NVARCHAR(30), SYSUTCDATETIME(), 126)
                ),
                2);

        -- Pseudonymize main customer record
        UPDATE dbo.Customers
        SET
            Email       = @ErasureToken + N'@erased.invalid',
            FullName    = N'[ERASED]',
            DateOfBirth = NULL,
            NationalID  = NULL,
            UpdatedAt   = SYSUTCDATETIME()
        WHERE CustomerID = @CustomerID;

        -- Pseudonymize address data
        UPDATE dbo.CustomerAddresses
        SET
            StreetLine1 = N'[ERASED]',
            StreetLine2 = NULL,
            City        = N'[ERASED]',
            PostalCode  = N'[ERASED]'
        WHERE CustomerID = @CustomerID;

        -- Mark request complete
        UPDATE dbo.ErasureRequests
        SET
            CompletedAt = SYSUTCDATETIME(),
            Status      = N'COMPLETED'
        WHERE RequestID = @RequestID;

        -- Record in audit log (token only, no original PII)
        INSERT INTO dbo.ErasureAuditLog
            (CustomerID, ErasureToken, ErasedAt, RequestID)
        VALUES
            (@CustomerID, @ErasureToken, SYSUTCDATETIME(), @RequestID);

        COMMIT TRANSACTION;
    END TRY
    BEGIN CATCH
        IF @@TRANCOUNT > 0 ROLLBACK TRANSACTION;

        UPDATE dbo.ErasureRequests
        SET Status = N'FAILED'
        WHERE RequestID = @RequestID;

        THROW;
    END CATCH;
END;

    Handling backups after erasure:

    After pseudonymizing the live data, backups taken before the erasure event still contain original personal data. Your backup retention policy determines the maximum window of exposure. For many organizations, the practical approach is:

    1. Document the erasure completion date in the erasure audit log.

    2. Note the oldest backup that pre-dates the erasure (and therefore contains the original data).

    3. Set a calendar reminder to confirm that pre-erasure backups have expired based on your retention period.

    4. For high-assurance environments, encrypt each customer's data with a unique DEK (Data Encryption Key) stored in KMS. To perform cryptographic erasure, delete the customer's DEK — all backups containing that customer's data become computationally unreadable even though the ciphertext persists.

    The DEK-per-customer approach is the most elegant technical solution for backup erasure, but it requires application-layer envelope encryption rather than relying on the database engine's built-in encryption — a significant architectural commitment that should be designed in from the beginning, not retrofitted.

    26.8 Operationalizing Sovereignty: Runbooks and Governance

    Technical controls are necessary but insufficient. Residency compliance fails in operations when an engineer under incident pressure creates a cross-region read replica to diagnose a performance problem, or when a backup copy is created in a non-compliant region by a script that predates the sovereignty policy. Governance structures prevent these operational violations.

    The residency violation response runbook:

    # Runbook: Responding to Data Residency Violation Alert
# Version: 1.0 | Owner: Cloud DBA Team | Review: Quarterly

## Trigger Conditions
- Compliance check script reports non-compliant resource
- AWS Config Rule "rds-no-cross-region-replica" fires
- Azure Policy compliance dashboard shows violation
- Manual discovery of misconfigured resource

## Immediate Response (within 1 hour)

### Step 1: Identify scope
- Determine which resource is out of compliance
- Identify what data is affected (classification level, jurisdictions)
- Determine when the violation began (CloudTrail / Activity Log timestamp)
- Identify who made the change (IAM identity from audit log)

### Step 2: Contain the violation
- Do NOT simply delete the out-of-region resource without preserving evidence
- Disable any replication sending data to the non-compliant location
- Revoke network access to the violating resource if it contains live data
- For RDS cross-region replica: promote replica (breaks replication), 
  then destroy the promoted instance in the non-compliant region

### Step 3: Notify
- Alert Data Protection Officer (DPO) within 1 hour of discovery
- Engage Legal if data has been accessible to non-compliant region > 24 hours
- GDPR: regulator notification may be required within 72 hours if personal
  data was accessed or if exposure constitutes a "high risk" breach
- Document notification timestamps in the incident record

### Step 4: Remediate
- Restore compliant configuration
- Re-run compliance check script to confirm remediation
- Review SCPs / Azure Policy for gaps that allowed the violation

### Step 5: Post-incident
- Complete incident report with timeline and root cause
- Update SCP / Policy to prevent recurrence
- Schedule training review if human error was root cause

    CI/CD integration for schema and migration residency checks:

    New database migrations can introduce sovereignty violations — adding a foreign key to a non-compliant table, adding a replication publication that includes new personal data tables, or changing a column type that bypasses Always Encrypted. Integrate residency checks into your migration pipeline.

    #!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
migration_residency_check.py
Pre-deployment check for database migrations that affect classified columns.
Run as a CI/CD gate before applying any migration in production.
"""

import re
import sys
from pathlib import Path

# Patterns that indicate a migration may affect personal data handling
SENSITIVE_PATTERNS = [
    (r'\bALTER\s+TABLE\s+\S+\s+ADD\s+COLUMN\b', 'new_column_added'),
    (r'\bALTER\s+TABLE\s+\S+\s+ALTER\s+COLUMN\b', 'column_type_changed'),
    (r'\bCREATE\s+TABLE\b', 'new_table_created'),
    (r'\bCREATE\s+PUBLICATION\b', 'logical_replication_publication'),
    (r'\bALTER\s+PUBLICATION\s+\S+\s+ADD\s+TABLE\b', 'table_added_to_publication'),
    (r'\bGRANT\s+SELECT\b', 'select_privilege_granted'),
    (r'\bGRANT\s+ALL\b', 'all_privileges_granted'),
]

# Columns and table names that are known to contain personal data
# In production, derive this from the classification metadata query shown earlier
KNOWN_PERSONAL_DATA_TABLES = {
    'customers', 'customer_addresses', 'customer_contact_preferences',
    'patient_records', 'employee_details', 'payment_methods',
}


def check_migration_file(filepath: str) -> list[dict]:
    issues = []
    content = Path(filepath).read_text(encoding='utf-8')
    content_lower = content.lower()

    for pattern, issue_type in SENSITIVE_PATTERNS:
        matches = re.findall(pattern, content, re.IGNORECASE)
        if matches:
            # Check if any known personal data table is referenced nearby
            for table in KNOWN_PERSONAL_DATA_TABLES:
                if table in content_lower:
                    issues.append({
                        'file': filepath,
                        'issue_type': issue_type,
                        'affected_table': table,
                        'requires_review': True,
                        'message': (
                            f"Migration affects '{table}' which contains personal data. "
                            f"Requires DPO sign-off and residency impact assessment."
                        )
                    })

    return issues


if __name__ == '__main__':
    if len(sys.argv) < 2:
        print("Usage: migration_residency_check.py <migration_file> [...]")
        sys.exit(1)

    all_issues = []
    for migration_file in sys.argv[1:]:
        all_issues.extend(check_migration_file(migration_file))

    if all_issues:
        print("RESIDENCY REVIEW REQUIRED:")
        for issue in all_issues:
            print(f"  [{issue['issue_type']}] {issue['message']}")
        print("\nThis migration cannot be auto-deployed. Submit for DPO review.")
        sys.exit(1)
    else:
        print("No residency concerns detected. Proceeding with deployment.")
        sys.exit(0)

    Key Takeaways

  • → **Managed services make decisions on your behalf** — automated backups, replication, and minor version patches all involve data movement. Audit every automatic behavior against your regulatory requirements before going to production, and use Service Control Policies (AWS), Azure Policy, and Assured Workloads (GCP) to enforce boundaries that survive operational mistakes.
  • → **Key custody is as important as data location** — a database encrypted with AWS-managed keys stored in the same region may still fail a strict sovereignty audit if your regulator requires that key material never be accessible to cloud vendor employees. Use customer-managed keys backed by CloudHSM, Azure Key Vault HSM, or Cloud KMS with Cloud HSM to maintain demonstrable key custody.
  • → **Audit logs are first-class residency artifacts** — if your audit logs are stored in a different region than your database, you have created a secondary residency problem. Configure CloudWatch Logs, Azure Blob Storage, and Cloud Logging to retain audit data in the same region as the database it describes, and include audit log residency in your compliance check scripts.
  • → **Right to erasure requires architectural planning, not just a DELETE statement** — personal data persists in MVCC dead tuples, WAL archives, backup files, and audit logs long after the row is deleted. Design erasure into your schema from the start using pseudonymization procedures and, for the highest assurance, per-customer envelope encryption with DEK deletion as cryptographic erasure.
  • → **Operational governance closes the gap that technical controls leave open** — SCPs and Azure Policies prevent many violations, but incident pressure, inherited scripts, and human error will occasionally produce residency gaps. A tested residency violation runbook, CI/CD migration checks, and regular compliance scans ensure that violations are detected quickly, contained, and documented in a way that satisfies regulatory incident response timelines.
